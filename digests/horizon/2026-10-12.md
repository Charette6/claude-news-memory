## AI & autonomous systems

### Researchers show a model can hide arbitrary capabilities that no evaluation can elicit
Slug: `provably-hidden-capabilities-elicitation-audit-limit` | lab result, moderate confidence (preprint, not peer reviewed) | origin: feed (arXiv cs.CR)

"AI Models Can Provably Hide Arbitrary Capabilities" (Draguns, Motwani, Douglas, Schroeder de Witt; submitted 9 October 2026, listed 12 October). Abstract: capability evaluations assume hidden capabilities can be elicited; the authors construct backdoor attacks that embed encrypted neural networks within host model weights, decrypting and executing them only on a secret trigger. Using "digital lockers", the hidden circuits are provably hard to elicit and interpret under cryptographic assumptions even with full weight access, extending prior work from hidden fixed strings to arbitrary computations. Proof-of-concept in PyTorch, tested against fine-tuning, weight noising, gradient-based trigger search and latent adversarial training on a small chemical-reaction prediction task used as a CBRN proxy; the authors report resistance to all four and describe the result as a limit of elicitation-based capability audits. Models released to support defence work. Bank read-across: evaluation reports and red-team attestations for third-party or open-weight models cannot establish the absence of a triggered capability; weight provenance and training-chain assurance are the controls that remain.

- `apex-harm-finance-agent-injection` (feed: arXiv cs.CR). "APEX-Harm: Evaluating Agent Safety in Shared Professional Workspaces" (Yang, Bibi, Li, Ghanem, Hinojosa; submitted 9 October). Benchmark of prompt and script injection against long-horizon agents doing finance, consulting and legal tasks over workspaces of 115 to 328 files with email, calendar, chat, document, spreadsheet, PDF and code-execution tools via MCP servers; six attack categories; 12 frontier models on 180 task instances each. Opus 5 and Sonnet 5 had the lowest overall attack success (8% and 20%); task-specific script replacement and attacker-provided generic script attacks averaged 81 to 84% and 73%; Gemini 3.8 Flash had the highest task score (0.877) with a 33% attack success rate. Authors' conclusion: a correct deliverable is not evidence of safe behaviour. https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13623
